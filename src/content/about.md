lede: Medicine taught me what clinicians need. Engineering lets me build it.

story:
  - >-
    I started in neuroscience labs at UCLA. I reconstructed about 400 neurons by
    hand for the Yang Lab's dendritome study (the same kinds of cells drifting
    behind this page) and analyzed burst firing across roughly 1,600 human
    neurons at Cedars-Sinai.
  - >-
    Then came medical school at the University of Maryland, and somewhere in my
    clinical rotations I realized I loved medicine more than I loved being a
    physician. I have deep respect for the profession, but I watched my
    classmates find a joy in it that I wasn't finding. So I asked myself what
    actually excites me. The answer was building: innovation, constant
    discovery, possibilities that never run out.
  - >-
    I'd felt that before, in the life science and neuroscience coding courses
    I'd taken. So, mostly for fun, I took Harvard's CS50 and Angela Yu's 100 Days
    of Code, and I kept building. Making something from the ground up through
    trial and error turned out to be frustratingly amazing. Then the AI boom
    pushed the possibilities wider than my horizon, and I realized my two loves
    didn't have to live in separate worlds. Health tech is where they meet. I
    graduated early with a Master of Health Science to build there full time, and
    for the first time I could see my future clearly. It's one I'd choose a
    thousand times over.
  - >-
    Today I'm the AI engineer at a pain management clinic, where I'm leading
    [Prelude](#projects), an AI intake system we're building with the clinic's
    physician. I also built [NotePilot](/projects/notepilot), a clinical note
    summarizer with a safety eval harness. I'm open to AI engineering roles where
    health or neuroscience meets software.
  - >-
    Off the clock, I'm at the gym, on a basketball court, or teaching myself to
    crochet, with slight success so far.
